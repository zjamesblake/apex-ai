After the Call

Everything from your call, in the order we ran it, in plain words. So you can run it again without me.

How to read this. Six steps. Each one asks you a question, tells you why it matters, and says what you need before the next one. One founder, Mara, runs a supplements brand and goes through all six with you, so there is always a real example to look at. The names we use for these ideas come at the end of each step, after you already understand the idea. Anything in a "more" box is detail you can skip the first time.

Step 1

Where does your time actually go?

Mara

Mara runs a supplements brand doing about twenty-four million. Asked where her week goes, she says "product and brand". Her calendar says something else: about forty ad creatives a week wait for her sign-off, some for four days.

Open your calendar and your inboxes for the last two weeks. Email, Slack, Discord, wherever the asks come in. Write down everything that repeats. Not what you think your job is. What the two weeks actually show.

For each thing on the list, write down three things:

  • How many hours a week it takes.
  • How much it drains you, one to five.
  • Whether it produces something, or is the chasing and checking around the real work. The sheet, the follow-up, the report nobody reads.

Then add two more things for each item. Who else could do the next bit, and why they do not do it today. And what is waiting while the item sits with you: a launch, a person, an order, a decision someone else needs before they can move.

Then name where the next profit comes from

Between now and the end of the year, where does your next step in profit come from? Profit, not revenue. One thing, with a number. And the single thing blocking it today.

Now go back to your list and mark each item: does it block that profit directly, does it just eat hours you would rather spend on it, or neither?

Why this comes first

You cannot fix what you cannot see, and memory describes the job you think you have. Mara would never have put the forty creatives on a list. Her calendar did. And if we do not know exactly what we are solving for, nothing we design later will fit.

Before you go on, you need

You should have a list of six or more things that repeat, each with hours and a drain score on it. You should have one sentence that says where your next profit comes from and what is blocking it. And from the list, you should be able to point at the two or three items that cost you the most. Those two or three are what the rest of this page works on.

What we call this

The list is your two weeks. The profit source is the lever. That is all the vocabulary this step needs.

Step 2

How much of your business only works because you are there?

Mara

Everything Halden does with AI has one owner. Jules drafts creator briefs with a prompt that lives in her own account. Support has a reply assistant one person built. In June, Jules worked out a hook that lifted click-through. She showed the two editors. Dev, who runs the ads, found out in August.

There are two kinds of ability in a company, and they feel identical from the inside.

The first is what a person can do. It shows up fast. It gets better when that person gets better with the tools. And it leaves the building at five o'clock, and for good the day they resign.

The second is what the company can do, with no particular person in the room. It grows slower. It is the only one of the two that is worth anything when someone values your business.

The test that tells them apart is one question: if you have to name a person for it to work, the company does not have it. That person does. You are renting it from them.

The trap is that getting three times faster yourself feels like the company got better. It did not. It got more dependent on you.

Five rungs

Companies sit on one of five rungs, from most dependent on particular people to least. Read them and find yours.

  • Chat. People use AI here and there in a chat window. Nothing is written down. Every improvement dies when the tab closes.
  • Personal. A few people have built their own ways of working. Real gains, entirely theirs. Your best person is faster and nobody else moved.
  • Shared. The ways of working belong to the company. Anyone can use them. Someone else's improvement made your Tuesday better and you never asked.
  • Delegated. The work runs without a person producing it. People check results, not drafts.
  • Self-improving. It got better this month and you can point to why, not to who worked hard.

Place yourself with two questions

  1. Take each thing your team does with AI. If the person who built it left, would it keep working?
  2. The last time someone on the team worked something out, how long until everyone else could do it too? Never, months, days, or hours?

Most companies are on Chat or Personal and describe themselves as Shared. That is not carelessness. Until recently, getting what people know out of their heads cost more than it was worth. What changed is the price.

Why this comes second

At your size, AI does not change how fast you go. It changes how much of the business depends on one person being there. Knowing your rung tells you what the next rung looks like, and that decides what kind of change we are looking for in your list from step 1. For Mara, the next rung is Shared: something she alone decides today, written down so that more than one person can run it.

Before you go on, you need

You should know which rung you are on, and you should be able to say the two answers that put you there. You should know which rung is next. And you should have the one question that gets you there. For almost everyone reading this, that question is the same: who else can do it, without you?

What we call this

A company where what one person learns becomes something everyone can do, without anyone making that happen, is AI-enabled. Ability that lives in a person is rented; ability the company keeps is owned. The one question is the Name Test. The five rungs are the Ladder.

Step 3

Can your current setup carry that?

Mara

Mara's creatives live in ClickUp and her notes on each one live in Slack. Anyone on the team could pull up the last forty in a minute. Her supplier process lives in her head and in a spreadsheet only she opens. Nobody could pull up anything.

Step 2 said the way up is to get a decision out of one person's head and into the company. That only works if the things the decision is about are somewhere other people can reach. A lot of good intentions die right here.

One question, for each of your top items

Could someone else pull up the things this decision works on, right now, without asking you? The creatives, the orders, the tickets, the numbers. Not your rules for deciding. Just the things.

  • Yes. They sit in a tool the team uses, or you could export them. Mark the item can move now.
  • No. They are in your head, on paper, in a chat nobody could search, or in a file only you open. Mark it needs a home first.

Answer it for each item on its own, not for the company as a whole. For Mara's creative approvals the answer is yes. The creatives sit in ClickUp, which the whole team uses, and anyone could export the last forty without asking her. So that item can move now; nothing about her current setup is in the way. For her supplier process the answer is no. The history sits in her head and in a spreadsheet only she opens, so anyone who needed it would have to come and ask her. That item needs a home before anything else can happen to it.

If the answer is no

Then the first job is small. Get every one of those things into one list the team already uses. A ClickUp list, a shared folder, a sheet. One week, no money. Done when the last two weeks of items are in it and new ones land there without anyone being asked.

If that would take more than a week, it is the wrong item for now. Pick another one from your list.

Why this comes third

Knowing your rung in step 2 tells you where you are going. This step tells you whether the ground under each item can take you there. Checking it now means you only spend effort on items that can move this month, and you know the small fix for the ones that cannot.

Before you go on, you need

Each of your top items should now carry one of two marks. Either can move now, because the things it works on are somewhere the team can reach, or needs a home first, with the one-week job of putting them in one list written next to it.

More: the four places things live, and two more fixes

The "no" answers come in a few kinds, and they matter later. In your head or on paper: memory, a notebook, voice notes, a chat thread nobody could search. Half and half: some in a tool, some not, no single place. The "yes" answers too: in tools, but separate: all digital, the tools do not talk, you could export it. Connected: the tools pass things to each other and something starts the work without a person asking. Only the connected floor lets a decision run on its own; the separate-tools floor is enough to share it, which is the step that matters.

Two more questions worth asking about a "yes" item. What starts it? Someone asking, a fixed time, or something happening in a tool. If it is "someone asking", give it a fixed time: nine every morning, or every Friday. Do you trust the numbers? If two sources disagree and nobody has picked one, pick one now and say why. And if the item needs two tools to talk, one export or one link between exactly those two tools is enough. Never a rebuild.

What we call this

The ground an item stands on is the Floor. If you were in St. Tropez, this is what the talk called workflow sophistication.

Step 4

Which one thing do you fix first?

Mara

Mara has three candidates that cost the most: approving creatives, re-explaining the brand to creators, and answering "is this claim okay?" in Slack. Her own words on the call: "Those three are the same problem wearing different clothes."

By now you have two or three items from your list that cost you the most, and you know which of them can move. This step is about choosing one of them to fix, and only one. To choose well, you first make sure each item is the kind of problem this method can solve at all. Then you ask two questions that rule candidates out. Whatever is left, you rank, and the top one is your pick.

Is it the kind of problem this method solves?

Everything this page can fix has the same shape. It is a decision you make again and again, on a stream of similar things, where someone is waiting for your answer each time. Approving creatives fits: the same yes-or-no call, about forty times a week, with Dev waiting on each one before he can launch. Reading the numbers every morning and deciding what to scale fits. Writing a brief for every new creator fits.

A big one-off call does not fit, however much time it takes. Deciding whether to rebrand, negotiating with a supplier, choosing whether to go into retail. Each of those happens once, and there is no stream of forty behind it. Those decisions are your actual job. The point of everything on this page is to take the repeating decisions off your desk so you have more time for the ones that only happen once.

Two questions that rule candidates out, then a ranking

For each candidate that fits the shape, ask two questions. A no to either one takes it off the list for now.

Check 1
Is it stuck with one person?

Is one person the only one who can do the next bit? If several people could already do it, it is not stuck with anyone, and there is nothing here to fix. For Mara's approvals the answer is yes. Only she can.

Check 2
Can it move now?

This is your mark from step 3. If the item can move now, or needs a home that takes a week to make, it stays. If getting it somewhere reachable would be a project, set it aside for now. Mara's approvals can move now.

Then rank
How much waits, times how much there is

Two things decide the order. How much is waiting on this decision: people, launches, money. And how much passes through it: how many items or numbers it handles. The more of both, the higher it ranks. If two are close, the one that costs you more hours and more energy goes first.

If two tie, pick the one whose written-down rules the other could reuse. Mara's rules for approving a creative are the same rules the creators keep asking about, so writing them once shrinks both problems. And if a candidate is chasing and checking rather than real work, try to stop doing it altogether before you write anything down.

Mara picks approving creatives. Forty of them a week pass through it, every launch waits on it, and it is the thing blocking her next profit. The "is this claim okay?" questions in Slack fold into the same pick, because they are the same rules asked a different way. Jules's week is real too, and it comes next, but not now.

Why one

One thing finished beats three things started. Three half-written documents are three more things that only work if one person is around. And the thing you pick here decides exactly what you write down in step 5, so pick it once and stick with it.

Before you go on, you need

You should have one item, chosen, and you should be able to say in a sentence why it came first. The next two are written down somewhere you will find them in January.

More: describe your one item in six parts

Useful before the hour in step 5, because the person sitting with you will ask for all six. What starts it. An item arrives, a fixed time, or someone asks. The stream. What it works on, and roughly how many a week. The decision. What you actually decide: yes or no, scale or cut, this creator or that one. Who decides today. You, or the one person. Who waits. The people, launches, or money sitting behind the decision. Where the lessons go. When the decision turns out right or wrong, where does that go today? Usually: into the head of whoever decided.

What we call this

A repeating decision on a stream, with someone waiting, is a loop. The two checks are the Two Gates. The one you picked is the one loop.

Step 4, continued

Which pattern is it?

Mara

Mara's one loop is a sign-off. Forty things a week wait for one person to say yes. She is not unusual. Almost every founder at her size has one of these, on creative or on purchase orders or on hires.

Every company is different. The places where work gets stuck with one person are not. There are six, and they show up whatever the department. Read the six tells, find yours, and open its guide. The guide tells you exactly what to write down, how to get it out of the head it lives in, how you will know it worked, and the one sentence to tell your team.

Things wait for one person to sign off on them before they can move. It might be creatives, launches, purchase orders, promotions, or hires.

Write down what you actually check when you say yes or no. Someone else runs that as a first pass. You look at what the first pass flagged instead of every draft.

We call this the Gate

You find yourself explaining the same thing again and again, to the team, to agencies, and to every new creator who joins.

Write it down once, in the place the work starts from, so every brief begins there instead of with you.

We call this the Echo

You read the numbers every morning and decide what happens today, whether that is scaling or cutting an ad, reordering stock, or moving cash.

Write down the lines where you act and what you do at each one. Then the recommendation arrives already made, and you only handle the exceptions.

We call this the Sensor

There is one person, not you, whose absence would stop specific things from happening. Their real judgment is wrapped up in hours of work anyone could do, so all of it lands on them.

Split their week into the part that needs their years of experience and the part that just sits next to it. Then write down the biggest thing in the second pile so someone else can do it.

We call this the Bundle

Someone on the team works something out that makes them better at their job, and it does not spread. They showed a couple of people. Months later, someone else finds out by accident.

Make one rule for lessons: where each one gets written, who writes it, and that it happens the same day.

We call this the Spark

Several people on the team are using AI in their own ways, there are no shared rules, and every "should I build this?" question comes back to you to decide.

Write one page for the team: the one problem you are solving, three to five rules that say no to things, and one speed limit. Then they decide from the page.

We call this the Fork

The guides

Open the one for your pattern. Each is about five minutes.

The Gate · things wait for one person's sign-off

What you will write down. The rules you use when you say yes or no. Some rules stop a thing outright. Some just flag it for a look. Some are close calls, where you write the ruling you made. And one line for what to do when unsure.

The one-hour conversation. You do not write this alone. The person who will do the first pass after you sits with you for an hour, with the last ten things you approved or sent back on the table. They ask "why did this one come back?" until it is on the page. When you hear yourself say obviously, you just, it depends, or you get a feel for it, stop. That is where the real rule is hiding. Prompt 3 from the toolkit runs this conversation for you.

The first run. Put the last ten real items through the rules and count how many come out the way you would have called them. Every miss is a rule you had not written. Add it the same day.

How you will know it worked. The first pass agrees with you on nine of the last ten, and nothing waits more than a day.

Nothing launches without the first pass and a named person who checked it.The one sentence to tell the team on Monday.
Finished example
Halden · creative approval rules · v0.1
Stops it
  1. No claim that a product treats, cures, or prevents a condition. "Supports" and "helps with" are fine. "Fixes insomnia" is not.
  2. No sleep claim with a number unless it is in the study we have on file.
  3. No before-and-after body photos on gut products.
  4. No creator says they are a doctor, nurse, or nutritionist unless we have checked.
  5. The product is named in the first three seconds.
Flags it for a look
  1. We sound like a calm friend who read the research. Flag "insane", "hack", "game-changer".
  2. One idea per ad.
  3. The problem comes before the product.
Close calls, with the ruling
  • "Wake up without the 3am spiral": fine. A feeling, not a claim.
  • A real review saying "cured my bloating": stop it. Using it is a claim.
When unsure

Flag it. Mara looks at flags once a day. Every time she sends something back, the reason gets written in here the same day.

The Echo · you explain the same thing again and again

What you will write down. One short page: who we are, who buys from us, what a good piece of work looks like, and what we never say. Short enough to read before writing a brief.

The one-hour conversation. The person who asks you most often sits with you for an hour, with the last five briefs you corrected on the table. Every correction you made is a line for the page. Prompt 3 with "writing a brief" as the task, or prompt 4.

The first run. Someone else writes the next brief from the page alone. Count your corrections. Each one goes into the page.

How you will know it worked. A brief written by someone else from the page needs no correction from you.

Nobody writes a brief from scratch. Every brief starts from the page.The one sentence to tell the team.
Finished example
Corvo Coffee · who we are · v0.1
Who we are

Specialty coffee for people who used to buy from a roaster and now have kids. We do the choosing. You do the drinking.

Who buys
  • Thirty-five to fifty, comfortable, and the morning is the only quiet hour.
  • Has tried a subscription before and cancelled because the bags piled up.
  • Buys on taste first, then on not having to think.
What good looks like
  • Specific over lyrical. "Chocolate and orange peel" beats "a journey".
  • The person, not the bean, is the hero of the first three seconds.
  • One proof per ad: the roast date, the pause button, the 4.9 from 3,100 reviews.
What we never say
  • "Artisan." "Curated." "Elevate."
  • Anything about caffeine as a performance drug.
  • Names of competitors.
Who keeps it

The founder. When she corrects a brief, the correction goes in here the same day.

The Sensor · you read the numbers and decide the day

What you will write down. The numbers you look at, which source you trust for each, the line where you act, what you do at that line, and the exceptions.

The one-hour conversation. The person who runs your ads sits with you for an hour with yesterday's numbers on the table. "What would you have done this morning, and why?" Prompt 3 with "the morning call" as the task.

The first run. Tomorrow, they write the recommendation from the page before you look. Compare. Every disagreement is a line you had not written.

How you will know it worked. Three mornings in a row where the recommendation matches what you would have done.

We don't scale or cut without the page. Exceptions come to me.The one sentence to tell the team.

The Bundle · one person whose absence stops things

What you will write down. Their real week, split in two piles: what needs their years, and what just sits next to it. Then the biggest thing in the second pile, written so that someone else can do it and get the same result.

The one-hour conversation. You sit with them, with their calendar and inboxes open, not their memory. For every item: "why did this come to you?" The useful answer is rarely "because it's hard". Prompt 2 sorts the piles; prompt 3 writes the big one down. You are not auditing them. You are giving them their week back.

The first run. Someone who is not them does the pile-two item next week, from the page.

How you will know it worked. Someone else did it, unsupervised, and the result was good. Not "the document exists."

If only one person can do it, it doesn't go live until it's written down.The one sentence to tell the team.

The conversation. You, alone, for forty-five minutes. Then the team. Prompt 4 drafts the page with you; prompt 5 tests every rule.

The first run. The next "should I build this?" gets answered from the page, not by you.

Finished example
Bramble Pet · the one page
The one problem

Cut the time from idea to live ad from eleven days to five without hiring. The person who owns this: the Creative Director, because it is her problem.

Rules that say no
  • We don't automate anything until we've tried to stop doing it. Stopped: the weekly performance deck. We killed it instead.
  • Nothing ships unless it takes away more work than it makes. Stopped: a dashboard that saved twenty minutes and cost an hour a week.

Speed limit

Nobody ships what they can't explain. How fast we can check things, not how fast we can build them, sets the pace.

More: where these usually sit in the life of an ad

For most brands your size, the candidates sit somewhere along the life of an ad. Deciding what to test and writing the brief. Getting it made. Approving it and launching it. Reading the results. And then connecting the results back to the idea and the person who made it, so the next brief is better. That last part is the one most brands skip, which is why the same lessons get learned twice.

Other parts of the business have the same kind of map. Order, ship, support, keep the customer. Buy stock, hold it, turn it into cash. Same idea, different stops.

Your pattern, and its guide open. From the guide: the thing you will write down, and the person who will sit with you for the hour.

What we call this

The six patterns are the Six Shapes: Gate, Echo, Sensor, Bundle, Spark, Fork. The life of an ad, from brief to results and back, is the Map.

Step 5

What do you actually do on Monday?

Mara

Mara's plan fits on five lines. Dev is the person. Tuesday is the hour. The approval rules are the thing. Every creative goes through the first pass before it reaches anyone, and nothing launches without it. Friday, the last ten go through and every miss gets written in.

Whatever your pattern, the plan is the same five lines. Put a person and a day on each. Every day lands before Q4 peak.

1
Name

The person who will make this decision after you. For Mara, that is Dev.

2
Book

The hour where that person interviews you, with the real items on the table. Mara and Dev, Tuesday, the last ten creatives.

3
Write

The one thing your guide says to write down. For Mara, the approval rules.

4
Set

When it runs from now on, and the one sentence you tell the team. Every creative, before it reaches anyone.

5
Run

Put the last ten real items through it and count the matches. Every miss becomes a rule. Mara does this on Friday.

Done means one thing, whatever the pattern: someone who is not you runs it on real items, without you watching, and the result is good. It never means "the document exists."

If step 3 said your item needs a home first, do that in the week before line 2: get everything into one list, then book the hour.

The one sentence

Every plan ends with one sentence you say to the team. It has to be a sentence that makes someone say no to something they wanted to do. It starts "We don't" or "Nothing ships unless". If it starts "we believe" or "we embrace", it is a poster, and nobody has ever refused anything because of a poster.

  • We don't automate anything until we've tried to stop doing it. Automate a bad process and you have made it permanent.
  • Nothing ships unless it takes away more work than it makes. Including the work of keeping it running.
  • If only one person can fix it, it doesn't go live. The whole point is fewer things that depend on one person. Don't add one.
  • Nobody ships what they can't explain. How fast you can check things sets the pace, not how fast you can build them.

Not sure if yours is a real rule? Prompt 5 asks it to name three things it would stop. If it can't, rewrite it.

Why five lines

A plan with names and days gets done. A plan without them does not. And one sentence the team can say back is what stops the thing you just wrote down from quietly going back into your head.

Before you go on, you need

You should have five lines, each with a person and a day on it. You should have one sentence you will say to the team. And you should have a date by which someone else, not you, is running it.

What we call this

The five lines are Your Five Steps: name, book, write, set, run. The sentence is the one rule.

Step 6

What is true by Q4 peak?

Mara
