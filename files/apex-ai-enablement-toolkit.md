# Where You Stand

*The diagnosis. Run this with your leadership team before you build anything.*

---

## The definition

Most definitions of "AI-enabled" are useless, because every company passes them. Here is one with teeth:

> **A company is AI-enabled when what one person learns becomes something the whole company can do, without anyone having to make that happen.**

Everything short of that is personal productivity. It goes home at five o'clock, and it leaves permanently the day they resign.

---

## The test

> **If you have to name a person, it isn't a company capability.**

Two kinds of capability feel identical from the inside and are opposites:

| | Personal capability | Company capability |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Outcomes **a person** can own | Outcomes **the company** can own with no specific person present |
| Grows when | Someone gets good with the tools | Learning persists and transfers |
| How fast | Immediately | Slowly, deliberately |
| At diligence | A liability | An asset |
| You | Rent it | Own it |

The trap: getting three times faster yourself feels like the company got better. It didn't. It got more concentrated. You didn't buy an asset, you paid to deepen a dependency.

---

## The five levels

Not a maturity model. A measure of how much of your business is rented.

**One · Chat**
People use it here and there. Every improvement dies when they close the tab.
*Tell:* "My team uses ChatGPT." Nothing persists.

**Two · Personal**
People build their own repeatable ways of working. Real gains, entirely personal.
*Tell:* Your best person is 3x faster and nobody else moved. You are now renting from one specific person, and they know it.

**Three · Shared**
Context and systems belong to the company. Anyone can use them.
*Tell:* Someone else's improvement made your Tuesday better and you never asked for it. First level where the gains belong to the company.

**Four · Delegated**
Work runs without a human generating it. Humans own the gates.
*Tell:* You review results, not drafts.

**Five · Self-improving**
The company improves how it improves.
*Tell:* It got better this month and you can point to the mechanism, not to who worked hard.

**The only jump that changes what your business is worth is two to three.** It is not a technical act. It is an organisational one, which means nobody can do it for you.

---

## The one number

> **How long between one person working something out, and everyone being able to do it?**

- Level one — never. The insight dies in a chat window.
- Level two — months, and only through hiring and training.
- Level three — days.

Measure it once a quarter. It is the only number here that cannot be faked and cannot be bought.

---

## The audit

Ask these in a room with your leadership team. Write the answers down. The value is entirely in how uncomfortable the honest answers are.

**1. Name the last thing someone on this team worked out that made them meaningfully better at their job.**
Who else can now do it? If the answer is "nobody" or "they showed a couple of people," you are at level one or two.

**2. If your best operator went on leave for six weeks, what stops?**
List it. That list is your rented capability, itemised.

**3. What does a new hire need a specific person for in their first month?**
Every item on that list is knowledge sitting in a head rather than in the company.

**4. Where does your actual operating knowledge live right now?**
Not where it is *supposed* to live. Where it actually is. If the honest answer is "in people, plus a wiki nobody opens," that is normal and it is also the whole problem.

**5. When something is learned the hard way, what happens to it?**
If the answer involves someone remembering to write it up, it does not happen. Reliably. At every company. Forever.

---

## What an honest answer looks like

Most teams answering these for the first time land on **level one or two**, and describe it as level three. That is not a failure of your team. Until about two years ago, getting knowledge out of people's heads was too expensive to be worth doing, so nobody did it. There was no business case.

That is what changed. Not the ambition. The price.

# Your First Five Steps

*Do these next week. None of them cost anything.*

---

## Step 1 — Name the person

Think of the one person whose absence next month would hurt the most.

You already know who it is. Everybody lands on their name in about two seconds, which tells you something on its own.

Do not start a programme. Do not start with tools. Start with one name.

---

## Step 2 — Split their week into two piles

Book an hour with them. One hour, not a project. Walk through what they actually did last week, task by task, and sort every item into one of two piles:

**Pile A — genuinely needs what only they know.**
The judgment. The call that requires the years.

**Pile B — everything sitting next to it.**
The admin, the checking, the formatting, the chasing, the re-explaining, the part where they redo something because the input arrived wrong.

### How to run the hour

- **Go by their calendar and their sent items, not their memory.** People describe the job they think they have, not the week they actually had.
- **Ask "why did that come to you?" on every item.** The useful answer is rarely "because it's hard." It is usually "because I'm the only one who can do the next bit, so the whole thing lands on my desk."
- **Do not let them be modest, and do not let them be defensive.** You are not auditing them. You are trying to give them their week back.
- **Write the two piles down where both of you can see them.**

### What you will find

Pile B is always bigger than either of you expected. That is the point.

> The bottleneck is almost never that someone's judgment is rare. It is that their judgment is **bundled** with four hours of work anybody could have done, so the whole bundle can only ever go to one desk.

Once you separate the two, the genuinely scarce part is a much smaller surface than it looked. Small enough to move.

---

## Step 3 — Take the biggest thing in pile B and write it down properly

One thing. Not everything.

"Properly" means: written so that a competent person who is not them can do it and get the same result. Not a summary. Not a Loom. The actual criteria, including the ones that are obvious to your expert and invisible to everyone else.

The fastest way to get it out: **sit with them and ask questions until it is on the page.** They cannot write it themselves, and it is not their fault. When you have done something ten thousand times, most of what you know has gone quiet. You need someone asking "and how did you know to do that?" over and over.

**Definition of done:** somebody else does it, unsupervised, and the output is good. Not "the document exists."

---

## Step 4 — Write your one page

Three parts, one page. Template is in the next file.

- **North star** — the one named, expensive problem you are solving. Not "we're going to use AI."
- **Guard rails** — what you will not do. Rules that make people refuse things.
- **Speed limit** — nobody ships what they can't explain.

---

## Step 5 — Give the team one rule

> **Nobody ships what they can't explain.**

That is your speed limit, and enforcing it is most of the job.

It will feel like you are slowing your best people down. You are not. You are stopping them building things the company cannot own — because the failure that gets you is not someone building the wrong thing, it is someone building past what they understand, and then it breaks and nobody in the building can fix it.

**Verification capacity, not build capacity, sets how fast a team can climb.**

---

## What not to do first

These are the three mistakes, learned the expensive way:

**"Everyone go watch these videos."**
Information without a problem to solve is entertainment. Everyone watches. Nothing changes.

**"Go build leverage in your role."**
No named problem, just go build. And once people can build, they build — because *"what could I build?"* is a far more interesting question than *"what is actually costing us money?"* You end up with a graveyard of half-finished tools and a team that feels extremely productive.

**"We'll reward usage."**
Measure activity and you will get activity. You need people rewarded for **getting rid of work**, not for producing it. Which means you need to be able to see outcomes rather than effort before you ask anyone to automate their own tasks away.

# The One Page

*North star, guard rails, speed limit. Copy the template at the bottom.*

---

## Why one page

Three things have to move out of you before you become removable:

- **What the company knows** — moved by writing your operating knowledge down in a form something can act on.
- **What the company can do** — moved by encoding outcomes so anyone can run them.
- **How the company decides** — moved by this page.

Most people do the first two and skip the third. Then their calendar fills up with *"should I build this?"* and *"is this a good use of it?"* and they discover they gave away the ability and kept all of the judgment.

> **Enabling your team does not reduce your decision load. It multiplies it, unless you hand judgment over too.**

You cannot write a rulebook, because this moves faster than any rulebook you could keep current. What scales is a short list of principles people can apply to situations you never anticipated.

---

## Part 1 — North star

**One named, expensive problem.** Not a tool, not a category, not "we're going to use AI."

The problem is the finish line. Without one you cannot tell when you are done or whether it worked, which is exactly how you end up with a graveyard.

Two rules that go with it:

- **The first builder is the person who has the problem**, not your most technical person. Every hand-off between the person who knows and the thing that gets built loses something.
- **Start with what the company knows, not with tools.** Tools built on no shared context are how you get the first failure mode.

---

## Part 2 — Guard rails

### The test everything comes from

Every tool anyone builds has to do at least one of these:

1. **Take work away.**
2. **Make the outcome easier to hit.**
3. **Make the outcome better.**

If it does none of those, you do not build it.

That sounds obvious until you look at what people actually build. Most of it produces a slightly better number and costs somebody an hour a week to keep running. **That is not a win. That is new work with a nicer interface.**

### What you are actually trying to delete

Most of the work in your company is not the work. It is the work about the work. Updating the sheet. Chasing the person who didn't update the sheet. The meeting about the sheet. The report nobody reads.

That is what these tools should be eating. Not your actual work — the scaffolding around it.

### The most important rule

> **We don't automate a process until we've tried to delete it.**

Automate a bad process and you have just made it permanent. It is faster, cheaper, it runs on its own, and now nobody will ever look at it again. You have taken something that was annoying enough to eventually get fixed, and made it quiet.

---

## Part 3 — Speed limit

> **Nobody ships what they can't explain.**

You can only safely own what you can verify. The gap that matters is not what someone can build — it is the gap between what they can build and what they can fix.

Cross it, and the company now depends on something nobody in the building can explain.

---

# Rules That Actually Refuse Something

*How to write guard rails your team will genuinely use, and how to tell when you've written a poster.*

---

## The test

> **A real rule makes somebody say no to something they wanted to do.**
>
> **If nobody has ever refused anything because of it, it is a poster.**

That is the whole test. Apply it to every line on your page. Any line that has never caused a refusal is decoration, and decoration is worse than nothing because it makes the page look finished.

---

## The pattern

Almost every rule that works starts one of two ways:

- **"We don't ______"**
- **"Nothing ships unless ______"**

If yours starts with *"we believe"*, *"we embrace"*, *"we leverage"*, or *"we are committed to"*, you have written a poster. Rewrite it as a refusal.

**The proof step:** next to every rule, write down the specific thing it has already stopped. If you cannot name one, either the rule is new (fine — come back in a month) or it is decoration (delete it).

---

## Worked examples

Each of these is a real refusal, with what it actually prevented.

**We don't automate a process until we've tried to delete it.**
*Stopped:* building a tool for a weekly report nobody read. We killed the report instead.
*Why it works:* automating a bad process makes it permanent. Faster, cheaper, runs on its own, and now nobody will ever look at it again.

**Nothing ships unless it takes away more work than it makes.**
*Stopped:* a dashboard that saved twenty minutes a week and needed an hour a week of maintenance.
*Why it works:* most builds produce a slightly better number at the cost of ongoing effort. That is new work with a nicer interface.

**We don't build for the job the way it works today.**
*Stopped:* a faster version of a check that should not have existed. We deleted the check.
*Why it works:* the job as it exists was shaped by a constraint that may already be gone. Speeding it up locks the old shape in.

**If one person is the only one who can fix it, it doesn't go live.**
*Stopped:* a genuinely good tool, for three weeks. It shipped with two people who understood it.
*Why it works:* the tool exists to reduce key-person risk. Shipping one that only one person can maintain moves you backwards.

**Every tool has a name on it and a problem written down, or it gets deleted.**
*Stopped:* eleven things.
*Why it works:* a tool with no named problem has no finish line. Never finished, never judged, never switched off.

**I'll only use AI in ways that get me off my computer, not further onto it.**
*A personal one.* Useful because it refuses a very seductive category: builds that are interesting to make and quietly increase how much time you spend at a screen.

---

## Examples that fail the test

These all sound fine. None of them has ever stopped anybody doing anything.

- *"We use AI to work smarter and move faster."*
- *"We embrace AI responsibly and thoughtfully."*
- *"We are committed to AI-first ways of working."*
- *"Everyone should be using these tools daily."*

The last one is the most dangerous, because it looks operational. It is actually the "reward usage" mistake in disguise — it measures activity, so you will get activity.

**Keep one failed rule on your page on purpose**, marked as such. It is the fastest way to teach everyone what the difference looks like.

---

## Prompts for writing your own

Do these in order. Twenty minutes with your leadership team is enough for a first draft.

**1. What have we built or nearly built that we regret?**
Every regret is a rule you did not have. Write the rule that would have stopped it.

**2. What is the most seductive wrong thing here?**
For most teams it is building something impressive that solves a problem nobody named. Write the rule that refuses it.

**3. What did we get away with once that we should not repeat?**
Near-misses make better rules than disasters, because nobody is defensive about them.

**4. What decision keeps coming back to me?**
Anything you get asked more than twice is a decision that should have been delegated. Write the principle that lets them decide it without you.

**5. Now go through each one and ask: has this ever caused a refusal?**
If not yet, keep it and check again in a month. If it never will, cut it.

---

## Three to five is the right number

Not ten. A page of ten rules is a page nobody reads, which means nobody refuses anything, which means you are back where you started.

Start with three. Add one when something goes wrong that none of the existing three would have caught. That is the only good reason to add one.
